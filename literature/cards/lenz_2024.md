**Publication status:** PUBLISHED

**READING_STATUS:** FULL_TEXT

**Evidence role:** HIGH-LEVERAGE SUPPORTING

## Scientific question

How do visual and GelSight tactile observations affect real-world RL peg-in-hole insertion, especially as clearance narrows and hole orientation varies?

## Exact system and regime

Real Franka Research 3 robot; parallel gripper with fingertip GelSight sensors; external Intel RealSense camera; modular base plate and peg. Dreamer-v3 policy receives 64x64 RGB scene and tactile images at 25 Hz and outputs relative Cartesian end-effector displacements at 10 Hz. Peg pose from OptiTrack is evaluation-only. Policies are trained at 2 mm or 0.5 mm hole tolerance (three seeds stated for 2 mm); evaluated in 20 trials across (tolerance, hole angle) = (2 mm, 0 degrees), (1 mm, 4 degrees), (0.5 mm, 0 degrees), (0.5 mm, 4 degrees).

## Main claims

### Tactile input improves performance in the tight-clearance regime

**OBSERVATION:** After 100k environment steps, every policy except vision-only trained at 0.5 mm completed training insertion (reward >500). In evaluation, all reported policies reliably solved 2 mm and 1 mm conditions, whereas at 0.5 mm success dropped for all except the vision-tactile policy trained at 0.5 mm, which retained strong performance.

**AUTHOR INTERPRETATION:** Touch complements vision where tight tolerance produces jamming/tilting and vision alone is insufficient for robust insertion.

**INFERENCE:** The reported advantage is regime- and training-distribution-specific; the results support a learned multimodal policy in this platform, not a general guarantee for tactile sensing.

**Intervention or measurement:** Train vision-only versus vision+tactile Dreamer-v3 policies at two clearances; evaluate success rate and mean length of successful rollouts across four hole conditions.

**Observed result:** The tight 0.5 mm vision+tactile policy was the only described policy with strong 0.5 mm evaluation performance; successful insertions took longer when jamming/tilting was more frequent.

**Causal strength:** INTERVENTION EVIDENCE

**Controls:** Vision-only policies; two training clearances; four evaluation difficulty conditions; 20 trials per reported final-performance condition; three seeds stated for 2 mm training.

**Alternative explanations not excluded:** Different training tolerance confounds sensing modality with training distribution for cross-condition comparisons; paper does not report uncertainty intervals or a multi-seed result for every condition in the text; tactile input may be correlated with other policy-learning effects.

**Scope limitations:** One peg/hole platform, limited clearances and angles (to 4 degrees), learned Dreamer-v3 policies, and constrained workspace.

**Source pointer:** Section 3, “The impact of tactile feedback during learning,” Fig. 2; https://arxiv.org/html/2410.23860

### Visual and tactile observations have axis-specific model attributions

**OBSERVATION:** Exact Shapley analysis of four action-prediction inputs (previous hidden state, vision, tactile input, previous action) on one exemplary vt-0.5 trajectory found hidden state predominant for x, vision most contributory for y, and touch most contributory for z.

**AUTHOR INTERPRETATION:** Vision guides peg–hole alignment in the camera plane; tactile feedback is crucial for vertical control because z motion is associated with tilts/jams and higher contact forces. Camera orientation may explain weak direct visual/tactile influence on x.

**INFERENCE:** These attributions describe this trained policy and trajectory; they do not directly measure sensor information content or establish that tactile signals encode force causally.

**Intervention or measurement:** Mask each of the four policy inputs with a default placeholder; compute all 16 Shapley feature combinations for the action output.

**Observed result:** Axis-dependent Shapley contributions in Fig. 3, on one trajectory in the most challenging condition (0.5 mm, 4 degrees).

**Causal strength:** CORRELATIONAL

**Controls:** Exact enumeration of all feature subsets (feasible because N=4); compares vision, touch, recurrent hidden state, and prior action.

**Alternative explanations not excluded:** Attribution depends on the masking baseline, feature dependence, a single trajectory, camera pose, and the learned recurrent representation; no ablation demonstrates an axis-specific causal sensor role.

**Scope limitations:** One policy/trajectory and one hardest-hole configuration; no statistical aggregation across trajectories or policies.

**Source pointer:** Section 3, “Explaining the role of touch during an episode,” Fig. 3; Conclusion; https://arxiv.org/html/2410.23860

## Important controls

- Vision-only comparator against vision+tactile policy.
- Training at both low-jamming 2 mm and high-jamming 0.5 mm clearance; distinct evaluation holes include 1 mm and 4-degree orientations.
- OptiTrack pose is withheld from policy inputs, limiting privileged state access during control.
- Shapley analysis includes prior hidden state and prior action, avoiding attribution of all action dependence to current sensory modalities.

## Critical assumptions

- The success/reward definition and OptiTrack evaluation accurately represent completed insertion.
- Image-based GelSight signals provide usable contact information in the tested setup.
- Default-placeholder feature masking yields meaningful Shapley modality attribution for the recurrent policy.
- Training/evaluation conditions and 20 trials are sufficient to characterize performance differences.

## Limitations

Authors explicitly state that the explainability analysis is confined to single trajectories and would benefit from more analyses (including gradient-based approaches); trajectory stages could support statistical evaluation. They propose harder object classes (e.g., screws and light bulbs) and representation-learning optimization as future work. The paper reports a small hardware/task regime rather than object- or robot-general results.

## What this paper supports

In this real robot peg-in-hole setup, adding tactile images to visual input was associated with a strong advantage for a policy trained and tested in the tight 0.5 mm regime; the authors’ single-trajectory attribution analysis found different modality contributions by action axis.

## What this paper does not establish

It does not establish tactile superiority across insertion geometries, sensor types, robots, controllers, or object classes; a mechanistic causal account of how tactile signals improve control; a statistically general axis-specific modality decomposition; or that results transfer without task-specific real-world training.

## Explicit open questions

- Whether the attribution pattern holds beyond individual trajectories and under other explainability methods.
- How insertion-stage categorization changes the analysis and enables statistical evaluation.
- Performance on more difficult objects such as screws and light bulbs.
- How to optimize representation learning during policy optimization and reduce real-world interactions with pretrained visual/visuotactile models.

## Evidence concerns

**Replication:** The text states three seeds for 2 mm experiments, but does not provide an explicit replication count for the 0.5 mm comparison.

**Measurement limitations:** Success and rollout duration are summarized in Fig. 2; the available text gives qualitative “strong performance” rather than complete numerical values. Shapley results use one exemplary trajectory.

**Potential confounding:** Training clearance changes alongside modality across some compared policies; camera geometry and recurrent state can shape attribution.

**Statistical / experimental concerns:** No confidence intervals, hypothesis tests, or per-condition seed counts are stated in the inspected text. The attribution method masks inputs with a placeholder, whose distributional realism is not evaluated.
