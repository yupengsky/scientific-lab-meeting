# Rethinking the Role of Demonstrations: What Makes In-Context Learning Work?

## Metadata
**Authors:** Sewon Min, Xinxi Lyu, Ari Holtzman, Mikel Artetxe, Mike Lewis, Hannaneh Hajishirzi, Luke Zettlemoyer
**Year:** 2022
**arXiv:** https://arxiv.org/abs/2202.12837
**Published venue:** EMNLP 2022, pp. 11048–11064
**DOI:** —
**Publisher / proceedings:** ACL Anthology
**Publication status:** PUBLISHED
**Reading status:** FULL_TEXT

## Abstract
Across 12 models, replacing demonstration labels with random labels often barely reduces classification and multiple-choice performance. Ablations indicate that label space, input distribution, and sequence format explain much of the gain.

## Scientific question
Which properties of demonstrations causally contribute to in-context task performance?

## Main claims
- Correct input-label mappings are often unnecessary for the tested tasks.
- Label space, input distribution, and format are important drivers.
- Meta-trained models rely especially heavily on simpler contextual properties.

## Key observations
- Gold-label and random-label demonstrations perform similarly in many settings, both above no demonstrations (Sec. 4; Fig. 1; Tables 2–3).
- Removing or changing label space, input distribution, or format degrades performance in different task/model combinations (Sec. 5; Tables 4–7).
- Effects are tested across 12 models, including GPT-3, GPT-J, GPT-2, and MetaICL (Sec. 3–5).

## Evidence for major claims
Random-label intervention: Sec. 4, Fig. 1, Tables 2–3. Component ablations: Sec. 5, Tables 4–7. Meta-training comparison: Sec. 5.4. Discussion and caveats: Sec. 6.

## Observation vs interpretation
### Observed / demonstrated
- Randomizing labels in the supplied demonstrations caused small average changes under the reported tasks and prompts.
- Altering contextual format, label inventory, or input-text distribution changed accuracy.
### Author interpretation
- Models often exploit distributional and formatting information rather than learning the demonstrated input-label mapping.
### Inference
- The result weakens the claim that standard ICL performance is primarily online induction of the demonstrated mapping; it does not quantify the mapping’s contribution in all regimes.

## Important controls
- No-demo baseline; gold versus random labels; multiple model families/scales; variants preserving selected properties while changing others; classification and multiple-choice tasks.

## Critical assumptions
- Random labels preserve enough non-label prompt structure for the intended ablation.
- Reported tasks and prompt templates generalize to ICL broadly.

## Remaining alternative explanations
- Models may infer task mappings from pretrained knowledge, label semantics, output priors, or input distribution even when individual labels are randomized.

## Limitations
The authors note task/model dependence, imperfect isolation of demonstration properties, and that the results do not rule out learning from labels in other tasks or settings (Sec. 6).

## What this paper supports
Demonstration format, label space, and input distribution are measurable contributors to ICL behavior; correct labels are not always necessary for benchmark gains.

## What this paper does NOT establish
It does not establish that labels are generally irrelevant, that no task learning occurs, or an internal transformer mechanism.

## Explicit open questions
How models learn from demonstrations in tasks requiring genuine input-label abstraction and how contextual properties interact (Sec. 6).

## Evidence concerns
**Replication:** Many models are accessible, GPT-3 access is constrained.
**Measurement limitations:** Accuracy aggregates heterogeneous task behavior.
**Potential confounding:** Random labels can alter label-token priors and semantic consistency.
**Statistical / experimental concerns:** Ablations do not isolate all correlated prompt properties.
