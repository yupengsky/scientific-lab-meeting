# Target Selection for Saccadic Eye Movements: Prelude Activity in the Superior Colliculus During a Direction-Discrimination Task

**Authors:** Gregory D. Horwitz; William T. Newsome

**Year:** 2001

**arXiv:** None

**Published venue:** Journal of Neurophysiology 86, 2543–2558

**DOI:** 10.1152/jn.2001.86.5.2543

**Publication status:** PUBLISHED

**READING_STATUS:** FULL_TEXT

**Source URL:** https://faculty.washington.edu/ghorwitz/wordpress/wp-content/uploads/2018/01/horwitz2001_2.pdf

**Evidence role:** CORE

## Scientific question

How does prelude activity in macaque intermediate/deep SC relate to saccade-target choice and the strength of the sensory evidence used to make a direction-discrimination decision?

## Exact system and regime

Three extensively trained rhesus macaques (*Macaca mulatta*) performed a delayed two-alternative forced-choice random-dot-motion direction-discrimination task. Motion coherence (0–51.2%) varied difficulty; one response target (T1) was in the recorded neuron's movement field and the other (T2) was outside it. Single-unit recordings sampled intermediate/deep SC. The final quantitative database comprised 96 neurons selected for greater prelude firing before T1 than T2 choices; 704 cells were initially isolated, but this sampling ratio was explicitly not a population estimate. Full paper pp. 2543–2558, Methods; Results p. 2547.

## Main claims

### Choice-predictive prelude activity occurs before saccade execution

**OBSERVATION:** About one-third of screened SC cells showed target-specific prelude activity; in the selected 96-cell database, firing generally differed before T1 versus T2 choices. On error trials, activity tracked the selected target more closely than the stimulus-motion direction.

**AUTHOR INTERPRETATION:** This activity represents a neural correlate of the forthcoming target choice; a subset is suited for target selection.

**INFERENCE:** The recordings establish association with choice, not that these cells cause the choice.

**Intervention or measurement:** Single-unit firing; ROC-area “predictive activity” from 100-ms spike-count bins comparing T1- and T2-choice trials; error-trial comparison.

**Observed result:** Predictive activity rose after motion onset and persisted through the delay in example/population analyses; high firing preceded T1 and low firing preceded T2 regardless of correctness at low coherences.

**Causal strength:** CORRELATIONAL

**Controls:** Error trials dissociated motion direction from selected target. The ROC measure removes target-nonspecific activity. Delayed-saccade trials assessed relation to movement parameters.

**Alternative explanations not excluded:** Motor preparation, an upstream decision signal, or correlated target representations can produce choice-predictive firing.

**Scope limitations:** Three trained macaques; sequential single-unit recordings; a fixed mapping from motion direction to saccade vector; quantitative analysis selected cells by target-specific prelude activity.

**Source pointer:** Full paper pp. 2543–2545 (abstract, task, ROC measure), p. 2547 (sample), pp. 2552–2553 (error trials), p. 2555 and Fig. 19.

### Sensory-evidence strength modulates predictive activity, especially in direction-selective prelude cells

**OBSERVATION:** Predictive activity was stronger/earlier at higher motion coherence. Direction-selective cells (44 in the pooled analysis) showed predictive activity that increased with coherence and stronger pre-stimulus bias effects; non-direction-selective cells' prediction was weakly coherence-dependent. Coherence explained early firing-rate variance in 33/96 cells after saccade parameters were included in regression.

**AUTHOR INTERPRETATION:** Direction-selective prelude cells carry information about both the forthcoming target and the sensory evidence instructing the perceptual decision, making them appropriate candidates for target selection.

**INFERENCE:** The result supports sensitivity to task evidence within this SC population; it does not show evidence accumulation or decision computation occurs locally in SC.

**Intervention or measurement:** Manipulation of random-dot coherence; single-unit firing and ROC predictive activity; regression including endpoint, velocity, latency, and coherence.

**Observed result:** Higher coherence improved the ability to predict the chosen target from firing; coherence added explanatory power early in the trial beyond recorded saccade metrics.

**Causal strength:** CORRELATIONAL

**Controls:** Identical response targets reported decisions across coherence levels; saccade-metric regression; stimulus direction and choice were dissociated on error trials; passive-fixation direction selectivity classified subpopulations.

**Alternative explanations not excluded:** Arousal, task strategy, reward-history bias, and sensory/motor signals inherited from other areas. The authors report inter-monkey differences and discuss task-strategy variation.

**Scope limitations:** Coherence effects on raw firing differed across monkeys, including inverse correlations in two animals; classification relies on a companion passive-fixation study; no causal manipulation of recorded neurons.

**Source pointer:** Full paper pp. 2543–2545 (task), pp. 2548–2550 and Figs. 5–10 (responses/subpopulations), pp. 2551–2552 and Fig. 13 (saccade-metric regression), pp. 2555–2557 and Fig. 19 (interpretation).

## Important controls

- Motion coherence was pseudorandomized, and the same targets reported strong and weak motion evidence (pp. 2543–2544).
- ROC predictive activity isolates differential T1-versus-T2 firing from non-specific arousal/anticipation changes (p. 2545).
- Saccade endpoint, velocity, and latency were modeled; early coherence effects remained significant in 33/96 cells (pp. 2545–2546, 2551–2552; Fig. 13).
- Error trials separated stimulus motion from target choice; firing tracked chosen target more strongly (pp. 2552–2553; Fig. 14).
- Direction-selective versus non-direction-selective status was measured in a passive-fixation task described in the companion paper (pp. 2548–2550).

## Critical assumptions

- Motion coherence indexes sensory-evidence strength/difficulty in this trained discrimination task.
- ROC predictive activity can be read as an ideal observer's ability to predict the eventual target from a neuron's spike count, without establishing a biological decoder.
- Regression over the measured saccade parameters adequately controls motor-metric contributions within their narrow ranges.
- The functional distinction between direction-selective and non-direction-selective cells follows from response constellations, not a direct cell-type identification.

## Limitations

- The authors state that causal influence of direction-selective neurons on target selection is unknown; initial microstimulation efforts were inconclusive (p. 2557).
- Only three animals were studied; coherence–firing relationships differed across animals and may reflect strategy or arousal (pp. 2555–2556).
- Sequential, rather than simultaneous, recordings cannot test the proposed correlated/anticorrelated population-state dynamics (p. 2556).
- The 25-ms “streak index” aliases fluctuations above 20 Hz; conclusions about transitions depend on the assumption that they occur predominantly below 20 Hz (p. 2546).

## What this paper supports

Single-neuron prelude activity in macaque SC is associated with the selected saccade target before movement. In a direction-selective subpopulation, the association is modulated by random-dot motion coherence beyond modeled saccade metrics. These are neural correlates, with no direct causal test in this paper.

## What this paper does not establish

- That SC prelude neurons causally select the target or accumulate sensory evidence.
- That direction-selective and non-direction-selective prelude neurons are anatomically distinct cell types.
- That firing-rate fluctuations are circuit-state switches; simultaneous recordings were not obtained.
- Generality beyond trained macaque oculomotor direction discrimination.

## Explicit open questions

- Whether directional SC neurons exert a causal influence on target selection; authors identify microstimulation as a possible test (p. 2557).
- Whether trialwise firing fluctuations reflect circuit-level vacillation between choices; the authors propose simultaneous recordings as a test (p. 2556).
- Whether differences between SC and LIP/frontal responses reflect neural structure or changes in task strategy over time (p. 2556).

## Evidence concerns

**Replication:** The paper reports a similar qualitative relationship across three monkeys, with substantial inter-animal differences in raw firing-rate/coherence relations (p. 2555).

**Measurement limitations:** Single units were recorded sequentially; choice prediction uses experimenter-defined T1/T2 movement-field geometry and ROC analysis. The selected 96-cell set is enriched for target-predictive cells (p. 2547).

**Potential confounding:** The authors addressed recorded saccade metrics and error-trial stimulus/choice dissociation. Uncontrolled choice bias related to recent reward history and possible arousal/task-strategy differences remain relevant (pp. 2550–2551, 2555–2556).

**Statistical / experimental concerns:** The 96/704 fraction is not a population prevalence estimate because cells/protocols were not uniformly sampled (p. 2547). Regression and permutation analyses are reported; no causal perturbation yielded an interpretable result.
